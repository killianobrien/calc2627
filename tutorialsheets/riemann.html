Summation formulae

Recall the summation formulae for consecutive powers we used in the lecture.

  • \[\sum_{j=1}^n j = \frac{n(n+1)}{2},\]
  • \[\sum_{j=1}^n j^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\]
  • \[\sum_{j=1}^n j^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}\]

  1. Use the formula for the sum of first powers to develop a general formula for summing a finite arithmetic sequence with initial term \(b\) and constant difference \(a\), i.e.  \[ \sum_{m=0}^n (am + b) = \dots .\] Simplify the right hand side in an appropriate way.

  2. Assume for the moment that there exists a formula for the sum of fourth powers, giving a polynomial of degree \(5\) with rational coefficients, of the form \[ \sum_{i=1}^n i^4 = q_0 + q_1 n + q_2 n^2 + q_3 n^3 + q_4 n^4 + q_5 n^5, \quad (q_j \in \mathbb{Q}).\]

    • By sampling this formula for various values for \(n\), generate a system of six linear equations for the unknown \(q_j\).
    • The system should have a unique solution. Use Matlab or similar to solve for the \(q_j\), thus discovering what the formula is.
    • How might we prove that this formula holds for all \(n \geq 1\)?

Partitions

  1. Consider a general interval \([a,b]\) of real numbers, where \(a \lt b\). Write down the general formula for the equally-spaced partition of \([a,b]\) into \(n\) sub-intervals. \[x_i = \dots \quad (0 \leq i \leq n).\] Then give formulae for the different possible strategies for choosing the representative points \(x_i^*\) using the left-hand edge points, right-hand edge points or mid-points of the sub-intervals, \[x_i^* = \dots \quad (1 \leq i \leq n).\]

A Riemann integral

  1. Set up and evaluate a Riemann integral for the definite integral \(J\), using equally spaced partitions and your preferred choice of representative points,
    \[J = \int_{-2}^1 3x^2 \, dx.\]
